Ten Simple Rules for Reproducible Computational Research

Reading

  • Title: Ten Simple Rules for Reproducible Computational Research
  • Author(s): Sandve G.K., Nekrutenki A., Taylor J., Hovig E.
  • Year: 2013
  • Journal: PLOS Computational Biology 9(10): e1003285
  • https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1003285

Why are we here?

  • Beyond Morality: Reproducibility isn’t just a moral obligation to your field; a lack of tracking is a direct technical burden on you.

  • The Time-Saver: Meticulous tracking allows your future self to effortlessly apply past methods to new data or reuse code templates across separate projects.

  • The Bare-Minimum Threshold: At an absolute baseline, you must at least be able to reproduce your own results to defend your science against future questioning.

  • The Reality Check: Because any given analysis contains an exponential combination of parameters and software variables, memory will fail you. You must record details while underway.

Rules 1 & 2: Eliminating Human Drift

  • The Documentation Trap: Manual logs easily drift out of sync.

  • The Executable Solution: Write your entire workflow as a directly executable file—such as a shell script or a Makefile.

  • Ban Manual Clicks: Avoid manual copy-pasting, mouse clicks or graphical format tweaks between documents.

Rules 3 & 4: Freezing Your Software State

  • The Version Lock: Minor changes between software updates can alter data outputs or prevent code from running entirely.

Rule 5: Exposing the Pipeline’s Inner Mechanics


The Analytical Value

Quickly browsing through step-by-step intermediate output files can reveal silent logical discrepancies, catching bugs that are completely masked in final summary charts.

The Pipeline Value

If your full project execution is long or resource-heavy, archiving standardized intermediate states allows you to re-run localized parts of the process without starting over from scratch.

  • The Audit Baseline: Storing intermediate files allows external peers to critically examine your complete pipeline logic without needing all your local executables operational.

Rules 6 & 7: Perfect Replications & Data Foundations

Rule 6: The Random Seed Mandate

Many analyses involve stochastic element randomness. To achieve an exact procedural reproduction rather than just an approximation, you must explicitly record and pass your initial random seed.

  • Isolate Visual Tweaks: Store the data behind your charts.

  • The Scripted Plot: Preserve the exact code used to render a graphic.

Rule 10: Providing Public Access to Your Workflow

  • The Minimum Material: Submit your core data frames and raw source code scripts as mandatory supplementary online files upon publication.

  • The Review Advantage: Exposing your full workspace infrastructure increases reviewer trust, accelerates editorial turnaround times, and boosts your project’s future citation metrics.

  • The Open Stance: Always remain prepared to openly answer peer requests for deep architectural or parameter pipeline metrics.

Scenario 1

Your teammate creates a breathtaking, beautifully styled bar chart for a group presentation. However, when the professor asks to see the 10,000 raw data points that generated the third bar, your teammate sweats and says, “Uh, it’s on my old laptop.”

  • Can a highly styled chart have any actual academic value?

  • How should losing access to raw files be treated?

  • Should the student be accused of data fabrication?

04:00

A pretty chart without raw data trails isn’t science; it’s just creative writing/coding. There is not a lot of academic value in a breathtaking, highly styled chart if the baseline data supporting it cannot be produced on demand.

“The burden of proof.” In some professional environments, an inability to instantly back up a specific visual claim with its underlying numbers looks less like a mistake and more like data fabrication.

Scenario 2

A student keeps a beautiful, 100-page handwritten leather notebook. She tracks every single mouse click, every copy-paste action, and every manual data-cleaning step she performs in Excel with flawless precision.

  • Is the student’s work reproducible?

  • Is there any value in (or place for) hand-written notes?

04:00

A beautifully kept manual record still fails the core requirement of being an “executable description” that a computer can run and scale.

Manual copy-paste actions and Excel mouse clicks introduce silent, untraceable human errors that version control cannot catch. We should prefer automated scripts that require zero human intervention to repeat.

There is immense value in handwritten notes and manual approaches, but only as a sandbox for human thought, not as the final pipeline for data execution.

The “Why” vs. the “How”: Code is excellent at tracking how a calculation is done, but it is terrible at explaining why you chose to do it. A handwritten notebook is a cognitive sandbox. It is where a researcher maps out messy conceptual logic, sketches potential workflows, and processes failures before writing a single line of script.

Scenario 3

You spend three weeks testing a hypothesis on a dataset. Your code is perfect, your pipeline is automated, but the results prove your hypothesis is completely, totally wrong. Your script yields a massive “dead end.”

  • Did your hypothesis fail, or did you?

  • Did you just waste 3 weeks of work?

  • Would you still share your work in a public repo?

04:00

  1. Did your hypothesis fail, or did you? It’s important to separate the quality of your code from the behavior of nature.
    Also, an automated “failed” experiment is actually a massive engineering success.

  2. Did you just waste 3 weeks of work? The danger of deleting your dead ends: What happens to the scientific community when researchers hide their failures, forcing the next person to waste weeks re-testing the exact same wrong idea.

  3. Would you still share your work in a public repo? Submit and store it with the exact same reproducibility standards as a success, because documenting “blind alleys” saves future researchers from wasting time on the same mistake.

Scenario 4

Your script runs a 4-hour simulation on a large dataset, processing everything live in temporary memory to output a final summary paragraph. When your professor spots an anomaly and asks to see the data right after the initial filtering step, you realize nothing was saved along the way.

  • What are some advantages of a single “all-or-nothing” live run?

  • What are some disadvantages of saving intermediate steps?

04:00

Disadvantages of a pipeline that only saves the final answer: impossible to troubleshoot, audit, or verify when a bug or anomaly inevitably appears in the middle.

Discuss the massive waste of time and computational energy that happens when you treat data analysis as a single “all-or-nothing” live run instead of caching milestones.

Debate the engineering trade-off between consuming hard drive space to store temporary milestone files versus consuming hours of processing time to re-run scripts.

Better to transform a 4-hour pipeline into a modular, flexible workflow that can be paused, inspected, and restarted at any point.

Poll

You run a classmate’s random simulation script on your machine using identical data inputs. But they forgot to set a static random seed. Your output values are approximately close (e.g., 84.2% accuracy) but not exactly equal to their published value (84.5%). What is your scientific verdict?

  1. Acceptable Replication: Close enough is good enough for computational science; minor stochastic fluctuations don’t undermine the core theory.

  2. Unverifiable Black Box: An approximate match is a failure; without an exact mathematical match via a random seed, I cannot verify if the pipeline is truly bug-free.

  3. Indifferent Stance: I assume the difference is caused by our different operating system kernels or processor architectures anyway, so a seed won’t fix it.
